## The moment | Small shows are almost impossible to find

Live, local music is everywhere and almost impossible to find. Buskers, pop-ups,
secret sets and small-venue gigs happen all around you and vanish without a
trace. ARND ("around") answers one question the big platforms don't: what's
playing near me, tonight?

## What I found | The app only works if people believe the show is real

This started as a design exercise in reputation systems and two-sided
marketplaces. The whole promise of the product is "this is really happening,"
so the thing to design for was credibility.

## The call | A small number of real shows, picked by hand

The obvious move was to scrape aggregators and launch with thousands of
listings. I chose the opposite: a small number of real shows, picked by hand,
every week. Community listings wear a badge saying what they are, and performers
claim their page through a reviewed Instagram handshake that ends in an Official
mark.

The same rule runs through the details. There are no fake success states: demo
content is labeled, counts are measured or say "syncs soon," and features that
live only on your device tell you so. Tips go person-to-person through the
artist's own CashApp, Venmo, or PayPal. ARND takes nothing and has no payment
rail of its own.

## What changed | A working app on live Toronto listings

The feed, map, and search run on live curated Toronto listings served from
Supabase, with row-level security on every table and hardened write paths. There
is a front door at arnd.app, a governed design system with five themes measured
to AA contrast, first-party crash reporting, and a written weekly routine for
keeping the listings real. It is in beta, and it says so.

The newest surface skips the venue entirely. Anyone can throw a listening night
from their profile: a shareable invite with RSVPs and plus-ones counted against
the cap, the exact address unlocked only for people who said they're in, and a
share poster that carries only the neighbourhood.

wordmark, and cards that route fans, musicians, and venue owners each to their
own path. It leads with a thank-you to the Toronto listening rooms that inspired
the whole thing.

Each community show carries cover art in the app's sticker-block language, made
per event: a park series finale, a Herbie Hancock listening night, a trip-hop
session.

The part I care about most is the one anyone can check. ARND keeps a public
changelog: over a hundred updates in plain language, each with what shipped and
why. Next to it are a design system page, where the code is the source of truth
and disagreements get fixed the same day, and an atlas of every screen in the
app. An app this size doesn't need any of it. I keep it so someone else could
pick the project up.

The iOS build is carried through App Store readiness: Sign in with Apple, in-app
account deletion, a privacy manifest, and accessibility work across VoiceOver
and Dynamic Type. It's live in Toronto, in beta.

## Looking back | What's next

ARND is the deepest thing I've built end to end, with Claude Code: brand,
product, backend, security posture, payments stance, content operations, and the
documentation that would let someone else pick it up. Next is the App Store
release.

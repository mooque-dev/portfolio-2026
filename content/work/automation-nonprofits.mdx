## Context

Nonprofit staff wear too many hats. Repetitive operational tasks — thank-you emails, donor record updates, follow-up triggers — eat hours that should go toward mission work.

Market research showed automation was the **#1 requested feature category** and a growing competitive gap. I partnered with Product and Sales leadership to frame this as more than a feature: it was a platform capability that would unlock future surface area for integrations, AI-assisted workflows, and custom reporting.

## The Research Surprise

I talked to 15 nonprofit operations managers and expected to hear about complexity concerns. Instead, the #1 fear was much simpler:

> "What if I accidentally email all 5,000 of our donors?"

That insight reshaped the entire design. The problem wasn't complexity — it was **confidence**. People needed to trust the system before they'd use it.

We also identified three tiers of automation needs: simple triggers ("when X, do Y"), conditional workflows with branching, and scheduled operations. 80% of needs fell into tier 1, but I advocated for building the tier 2/3 architecture from day one. It meant more engineering investment upfront, but avoided a costly rewrite later.

## The Design

### Visual Workflow Builder

A node-based canvas that makes automation logic tangible. Users see their automation as a visual flow — trigger, conditions, actions — instead of filling out abstract forms. The builder deliberately constrains complexity: start from a template, extend it. No blank canvas anxiety.

### Preview Mode (The Key Feature)

Before activating any automation, users can preview exactly which contacts it would affect. This was the feature that gave people the courage to actually turn things on. They could verify without risk. This design decision directly traces back to that research insight about the 5,000-donor nightmare.

### Progressive Complexity

New users see a curated template library. As they build confidence, the interface reveals more — custom triggers, conditional logic, webhook integrations. The system grows with the user. Same philosophy as Orchid's contribution model: meet people where they are.

## Beyond Design

I worked with Sales and Marketing on launch positioning, created demo flows for the sales team, and ran training sessions for Customer Success. Good product design doesn't stop at the Figma file — it includes the story you tell about the product.

## What Happened

- **45% of active accounts** created an automation within 3 months
- Teams saved an average of **4.2 hours per week**
- 78% of automations started from templates (validating the template-first approach)
- Became a key differentiator in competitive sales conversations
- Architecture now supports the AI-assisted workflow roadmap

The preview mode insight — building for confidence, not just capability — is something I keep coming back to. It applies to design systems, data integrations, and really any feature where users fear irreversible consequences.

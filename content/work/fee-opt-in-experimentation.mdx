## Stakes | The Deceptive Win

A redesigned fee box on Raisely had just lifted opt-in from **75% to 82%**, near the **90% benchmark**. But the **12–20% of donors who clicked "Edit"** mostly dragged the fee to the minimum or opted out. Awareness wasn't the problem. The opportunity was **<mark class="pen" data-note="the whole case, in two words">decision design</mark>**: the few seconds after a donor decides to change something.

## Reframe | Reframing the Problem

The easy move was to ship the 82%. I argued to pause: the edit box dropped donors into an older fallback that **nudged them toward the smallest contribution**. The new question: how do we make the easiest path a fair one, without it ever feeling coercive? <mark class="pen" data-note="the line I argued from">Donor trust is the product.</mark>

## Experiment | Designing the Decision, Not the Default

I designed three variants grounded in defaults, anchoring and soft-decline (a slider, toggle buttons and a segmented dropdown) and tested them against the existing control in Pendo. Each was built production-ready, so the winner could ship platform-wide the day the test closed.

## Hypothesis | The Hypothesis

> Introducing a modernized fallback control when a donor clicks "Edit" will increase the average fee opt-in value while maintaining or improving the existing conversion rate.

## Result | What Happened

The **Slider** lifted fee opt-in from **75% to 92%**, past the 90% benchmark, while conversion and average gift size held steady. It shipped as the fallback across the platform.

<figure class="wide">
  <img class="clear" src="/case-studies/fee-opt-in/05-results.svg" alt="Fee opt-in increased from 75 percent to 92 percent" />
  <figcaption>The winning Slider variant cleared the 90% benchmark, without trading away conversion or average gift size.</figcaption>
</figure>

> Improving *how* a user changes their decision can be just as effective as improving the default itself.

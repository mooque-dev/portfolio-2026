## The Situation

After Keela's acquisition by Aplos, three products needed to become one. Each had its own component libraries, color palettes, spacing scales, and interaction patterns. The inconsistency was visible to users and frustrating for the teams building across products.

But here's what I recognized early: **this wasn't a design system problem — it was a culture problem.** The real challenge was getting three teams with different histories and priorities to build on shared foundations. You can't mandate that.

## Making the Case

Previous design system efforts at the company had died because they couldn't secure dedicated engineering time. I pitched Orchid differently — not as a "UI refresh" but as a **force multiplier**. The deck I presented to leadership tied the investment to three numbers: faster feature delivery, less QA rework, and a coherent experience that would smooth the product merger.

It worked. We got two dedicated frontend engineers — the first time a design system had earned that commitment.

## How We Built It

### The Audit

I led a comprehensive inventory across all three products: 340+ unique components. Through workshops with each team (not presentations *to* them), we distilled those down to 86 foundational primitives covering 95% of interface needs. The workshops mattered as much as the output. Every team had a voice in the system they'd be living with.

### Tokens First

Orchid is built on a token foundation. Colors, spacing, typography, elevation, and motion are all defined as tokens flowing from Figma variables to code constants. This architecture enables theming, ensures design-dev parity, and provides the backbone for the eventual product unification.

### A Contribution Model That Actually Works

My first attempt at governance was too formal — proposal templates, committee reviews, multi-day turnarounds. Contribution proposals dropped to near zero. So I simplified: async reviews, 48-hour turnaround, lightweight templates. Proposals doubled overnight. Governance should reduce friction, not create it. That was a hard lesson.

## Getting Adoption Without Authority

I had no direct authority over most of the people who needed to adopt Orchid. This is the part of the work I find most interesting. What worked:

- **Weekly office hours** — drop-in sessions where anyone could get help migrating or propose components. These built relationships and gave me real-time signal about what was actually hard for people.
- **Paired adoption sprints** — I sat with engineers during migration, pair-designing and debugging together. Teams adopted because Orchid was genuinely faster, not because it was required.
- **Migration guides for each product** — meeting each team where they were, not where I wanted them to be. The guide for each product looked different, because each product's codebase was different.

## What Happened

- **40% faster** component development for new features
- Adopted across all three products within 6 months
- 96% component reuse rate in new feature development
- 70% reduction in visual inconsistency bugs
- 12+ designers and engineers contributing monthly
- Orchid is now the foundation for the unified product experience

The contribution model is the thing I'm proudest of. A design system nobody contributes to is just a style guide with extra steps.

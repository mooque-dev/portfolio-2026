## The moment | Three products, three looks, and teams who didn't choose each other

After the acquisition in 2025, three products with three visual languages had to become one. I saw early that most of the work would be cultural: three product teams with different histories had to choose to build on shared foundations.

Between them, the three products ran on nine design systems, each with its own colours, spacing and patterns. Values were set by hand, down to decimal measurements that never matched between design and code, and the inconsistencies were often found after release, by customers.

It worked: we got two dedicated frontend engineers, the first time a design system had earned that.

I ran the audit myself over about three weeks and tracked it in Figma and Notion. Engineers from all three products helped me find the one-offs and inconsistencies that were really in the code, and told me what worried them about building the shared versions. Most of the 340+ turned out to be the same thing built more than once. The dropdown alone existed in nine variations. They became one select with four variants (single, multiple, searchable, and with icons) plus a separate date picker. Address and country lookups became the searchable variant with preset lists, and the two that only looked like dropdowns went away.

## The call | One component per job, and a test for everything else

We folded duplicates into one component with variants, wrote the rules for when to use each, and cut the one-offs nobody needed. That is how 340+ became 70, and it covered the money parts too: forms and receipts.

The call had a cost. My components were roomier and friendlier, and the Aplos team pushed back hardest: on their accounting screens that cost space and got in the way of workflows people already knew. They also had a fair point about tone: accounting software that looks playful doesn't serve the people who trust it with their books. So Orchid kept one set of components and carried a variation for each product, Keela, Raisely and Aplos, plus a neutral white-label one, with usage guidelines and notes on where each component was used. In the end Aplos adopted most of it.

I ran workshops with each team to build the 70 on tokens, with Storybook as the source of truth and Chromatic catching regressions on every pull request.

I had no authority over most of the people who needed Orchid, so adoption had to be earned: weekly office hours, pairing with engineers during migration, a migration guide per codebase, and bi-weekly stakeholder reviews. Teams adopted it because it made their work faster.

I got governance wrong first. Contributing meant filling in a form and bringing it to a weekly meeting, and I was building an approval step on top. Proposals dropped to near zero. The signs were everywhere once I looked: an empty queue, the same questions passed along in broken-telephone threads across Slack channels, no single source of truth, and engineers quietly building their own components. <mark class="pen" data-note="my mistake, and I fixed it">The formality was the problem</mark>, so I dropped the approval step and rebuilt around 48-hour async reviews and light templates. Proposals doubled.

Orchid was adopted across all three products, four engineering teams now build with it, and it is the foundation of the unified experience. Along the way it earned two dedicated front-end engineers, and contribution proposals doubled once reviews went async.

## Looking back | What I'd do differently

I'd bring Aplos engineers into the audit in week one, so the system was partly theirs before I asked them to adopt it. And I'd measure adoption per product from the start, so I could show progress in numbers and not only in conversations. And I'd set up one place from day one where anyone could log a problem with a screenshot and a comment, so the system had a single source of truth before the Slack threads started.

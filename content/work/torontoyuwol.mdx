## Why it exists

In 2025 I joined an amateur musical company, well outside my comfort zone, and
found the operational chaos every volunteer group knows: months of rehearsals,
about forty people across cast, production, and promotion teams, two languages,
and everything coordinated through group chat. The people were committed. The
tools were a spreadsheet nobody opened and a chat nobody could search.

So I started building, one annoyance at a time.

## One tool became a suite

**Scheduling and attendance.** A shared calendar of practices and performances,
with attendance tracked per date, so who's in the room is a glance instead of a
thread. People pick their team and their language; the whole thing runs in
Korean and English because the company does.

**A bilingual script reader.** Rehearsing a translated show means half the room
works from Korean and half from English. The reader keeps both scripts in one
place, used daily through the run, which quietly made it the most-loved thing I
built for them.

**Ticketing, with real money.** When the production needed to sell seats, I
built that too: performance browsing, seat selection, payment, and
confirmation, shipped and used to sell tickets for the ten-month run. Designing
a payment flow for your own community, where every buyer might sit next to you
at rehearsal, is a very effective accountability structure.

## Designing for people who see you every week

This is internal tooling in its purest form: a non-technical user base of real
people I couldn't avoid, using the tools daily, telling me directly what was
broken. I iterated weekly on that feedback. No analytics dashboard substitutes
for the stage manager walking over to say the attendance view confused her.

Adoption was never mandated. It happened because each tool removed a specific,
named annoyance, and because the people it served watched it get better every
week. That's the whole playbook for internal tools, at any scale: earn the
workflow, don't decree it.

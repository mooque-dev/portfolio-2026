## The moment | A $200 gala payment the product could only call one thing

A supporter pays $200 at a gala. $100 is the seat, so they got something back. $100 is a gift. Keela could record a payment as one type only, so it saw a $200 donation or $200 of revenue, and neither was true. Staff logged it as separate entries or wrote to support, because otherwise the books and the tax receipt didn't match.

People had asked for a fix in their own words. In Productboard, 40 of 72 newly onboarded organizations (56%) raised it within their first 28 days, and about 500 support tickets a quarter were about transactions. It was our top churn driver.

## What I found | All eight kept a side spreadsheet

I sat with 8 organizations while they did their books. Some of the people doing it were accountants, and many weren't. All 8 kept a side spreadsheet or a separate accounting tool, and they all went around the same loop:

1. Record the payment in Keela as several payments.
2. Track the real split in Excel.
3. Match one bank deposit to three records in the bookkeeping tool.
4. Go back to Keela and fix the receipts.

Nobody could see a mistake until the final report. So I defined the job as recording a payment the way it happened, with the total checking itself while they worked.

Talking to the organizations that had voted for this in Productboard showed the same thing. The split was the core problem, more than category labels.

<figure>
  <img src="/case-studies/transaction-workflows/image-1.png" alt="User problem statement: as a nonprofit admin I need to split a single payment into multiple items" />
  <figcaption>The problem statement that framed the work</figcaption>
</figure>

Each part of a payment also had to produce a correct tax receipt, and each country draws the line differently:

- **Canada.** When a donor gets something back, the receipt shows the total paid, the value of that advantage, and the eligible amount left over.
- **The US.** A payment over $75 that is partly a gift needs a written disclosure with a good-faith estimate of what the donor received.
- **Australia.** A payment at a fundraising event is deductible only if the benefit is minor: worth no more than 20% of the payment or $150, whichever is less.

The required fields came from legal and compliance research. My job was to make getting them right the easy path.

## The call | A full page, three steps, and a total that checks itself

Before designing, the PM, the engineering lead and I agreed on what we couldn't do. The product was a monolith, so there would be no backend rebuild, and the data model that reporting relied on had to stay as it was. I was the only designer on it.

<figure>
  <img src="/case-studies/transaction-workflows/image-5.jpg" alt="Phased delivery plan, stakeholder alignment on constraints" />

The first decision was to move the task out of a pop-up and onto a full page. I gave up seeing the list behind it. In return there was room for several parts of one payment side by side. It was cheap to build, because engineers reused the pop-up's system at full-page size.

What shipped is three steps, one decision per screen, so a first-timer always knows what is being asked. Each one answers something I watched happen.

The three steps have a cost. The campaign is chosen a step away from the split it belongs to, and the receipt only shows at the end.

I mapped every state of a split before build, including the two errors. When the parts add up to more than the payment, the screen says how much it is over in words, not only in red, and the payment still can't move on. A part with no amount says what to fix. Once a payment is logged, the contact is locked and deleting asks for a reason, so the audit trail holds. Other details can still be corrected.

It shipped behind a feature flag, next to the old screens. The split went to a beta group first, because everything else depended on it, then rolled out group by group with a switch to turn it off.

## What changed | One record where there had been three

For the people doing the books, a split payment became one record. When I tested the move from the pop-up to the full page with the 8 organizations, their rating of the flow went from **5.5 to 8.8 out of 10**, from a short survey after each session.

The details page took three rounds. The first changed too little and scored **6**. The second borrowed patterns from across the suite and scored **7.5**. The third put the items first and who they belonged to second, and scored **9.5**. That order is how people think about a payment: what did I record, then whose is it.

  <img src="/case-studies/transaction-workflows/image-11.png" alt="The transaction details page after the redesign: a $1,500 payment shown as separate items, a donation and revenue, each with its own details" />
  <figcaption>The details page after the redesign: each part of the payment is its own item</figcaption>

In the first month after launch, support tickets about categorizing payments fell **70%**, and manual adjustments, meaning edits after saving, fell **60%**. Receipts stayed correct and reports didn't change. About $1.8M a year in subscriptions sat with the accounts that needed this.

Read those numbers with care. This is a before and after over one month. We had too few organizations to split into a fair test, so there was no control group. Churn is read year over year, so I don't claim a churn result.

I added three pieces to the design system for this work: the split row, the bar under the total and the step layout. Teams on Aplos and the other products in the suite picked them up afterwards. Suggested categories became a platform rule I later wrote into Orchid's guidelines.

## Looking back | What I got wrong, and what I'd ship next

The first version of the details page was my mistake. I polished the old layout when I should have asked what people check first, and testing told me so. Next time I'd start there.

I also set up the measuring after launch. Next time I'd have a baseline ready, roll out to some organizations first, and use the flag itself as the test: organizations on it against organizations not yet on it, counted as tickets per 100 active organizations.

After the project I rebuilt the task two more ways to compare them with what shipped. One asks guided questions in plain words, which is slow for someone recording dozens of payments a day. The other is one line per part, all on one screen, which looks like the spreadsheet all 8 already kept. That second one is what I'd ship next, with a final check that shows what goes on the receipt. It isn't tested. I'd run it against the shipped flow with 6 to 8 people who do the books, and measure wrong receipts, time to finish and steps back.

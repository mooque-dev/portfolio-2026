## Stakes | Why This Mattered

Keela's transaction model handled one-time donations well and broke for larger nonprofits, where one payment is often a ticket, a donation and a campaign gift at once. Staff logged several records instead, which meant wrong receipts, messy books and support tickets. As the team pushed upmarket after the acquisition, it had become a growth blocker.

- **56%** of newly onboarded orgs (40 of 72) voted for transaction flexibility
- **~500 support tickets a quarter** tied to splitting, receipts and reporting

## Research | What I Learned From Watching People Work

I sat with 8 nonprofit accountants while they reconciled. Every one kept a parallel spreadsheet, one called batch editing "death by checkbox," and none could see a miscategorized transaction until the final report.

## Approach | Design for What Breaks Next

The easy path was a one-off split screen for the loudest complaint. I pushed for extensibility instead, so the next capability wouldn't need a rewrite. Before designing, the PM, the engineering lead and I agreed on what we couldn't do: no backend refactor, no breaking the data model behind reporting. We shipped in phases.

## Before and after | The Design

Four changes, each answering something the accountants showed us. Suggested categories became a platform rule I later wrote into Orchid's guidelines, and the new design <mark class="pen" data-note="the year-end surprise, gone">validates continuously</mark> instead of at report time.

<div class="tablewrap"><table class="changes"><thead><tr><th></th><th>Before</th><th>After</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><span class="n">1</span></td><td data-label="Before"><b>One type per payment.</b> A gala ticket that was part donation, part value received had to be logged as several records.</td><td data-label="After"><b>One payment, split by purpose.</b> Donation, in-kind gift and value received live in a single transaction.</td></tr><tr><td><span class="n">2</span></td><td data-label="Before"><b>Categorizing by hand.</b> Every transaction started blank, and accountants kept a parallel spreadsheet.</td><td data-label="After"><b>Smart defaults, human override.</b> A suggested category from donor history, amount and fund, accepted in one click.</td></tr><tr><td><span class="n">3</span></td><td data-label="Before"><b>"Death by checkbox."</b> A flat table where batch edits meant ticking rows one at a time.</td><td data-label="After"><b>Batch by context.</b> Scope by date range, donor, fund or category, then act on the group.</td></tr><tr><td><span class="n">4</span></td><td data-label="Before"><b>Errors at report time.</b> A miscategorized transaction stayed invisible until the final report.</td><td data-label="After"><b>Continuous validation.</b> Each reporting period shows its health as you go.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>

## Testing | Iteration Was the Work

I tested early prototypes with 8 organizations. Moving from a modal to a full-screen split workflow lifted satisfaction from **5.5 to 8.8 out of 10**. The transaction details view took three rounds: V1 changed little (**6/10**), V2 borrowed patterns from across the suite (**7.5/10**), and V3 put the items first and the associations second (**9.5/10**).

## Result | What Happened

In the first month, categorization support tickets fell **70%** and manual adjustments **60%**. The work supported **$1.8M ARR** in connected accounts, and one payment could finally be recorded the way it happened, compliant across Canadian, US and Australian tax rules. The most important artifact was the system map I drew in week one, not any Figma file.
